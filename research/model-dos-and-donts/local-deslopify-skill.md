# Deslopify

Make the copy sound like it came from a person who knows the subject and has a reason to write about it.

Good prose is accurate, specific, proportionate, and readable. It does not announce its intelligence or its warmth. It puts the important thing on the page, gives it enough context to be understood, and stops when the thought is complete.

## Start with substance

Before changing sentences, establish:

- what the piece is about;
- what the reader needs to know or decide;
- which claims the source actually supports;
- what would be lost if each paragraph disappeared.

If a paragraph has no claim, do not give it a more interesting surface. Flag the gap. If the requested length exceeds the available material, ask for more material or write a shorter piece. Interpretation, atmosphere, and praise are not substitutes for information.

## Build the piece

- Start with the subject, situation, or decision. Put the concrete thing on the page before discussing its significance.
- Treat text above a heading as metadata, not decoration. Use an eyebrow or kicker only when it gives the reader a real category, date, status, or navigation cue. If it merely restates the subject or adds atmosphere, omit it and put any necessary context in the opening sentence.
- Give each paragraph one job. A paragraph can establish a fact, explain a choice, show a consequence, or make a judgment; it does not need to perform all of them at once.
- Make the reasoning visible. Show what happened, what was tried, what changed, and why the result matters when the source contains that information.
- Let an abstract claim meet a specific example, mechanism, name, number, place, or consequence. Add specificity only when it is supported by the source.
- Keep interpretation close to evidence. State what happened and let the reader draw the first conclusion. Add interpretation when the piece has an argument to make, not to give ordinary facts a larger meaning.
- Allow paragraphs and sections to have different weights. A short observation may deserve one sentence; a difficult point may need five. Do not stretch or compress them for symmetry.
- End on the last sentence that adds information, judgment, or a necessary qualification. A piece does not need to summarize itself after the reader has finished it.

A paragraph needs a subject, a point, and enough evidence to support the point. The order and proportions should follow the material.

## Do not write the gloss

An **interpretive gloss** (also called significance padding) is a sentence that steps outside the subject to explain what the preceding facts supposedly mean. It adds a verdict about importance, personality, or the future instead of adding information or a defensible consequence.

The pattern is easy to recognize: a list of facts is followed by a claim that the list “says more” than it does, a project or product is given a temperament such as “comfortable moving” between categories, and the paragraph closes with a generic promise that the work is still changing. The prose sounds interpretive, but the reader has learned nothing that the facts did not already contain.

When the source gives you a list, write the list. If the list has a consequence, name that consequence precisely. If it supports an argument, make the argument and show the evidence. If it supports neither, stop. Do not turn variety into personality, unfinished work into optimism, or the writer's reaction into a fact about the subject.

## Make sentences carry their weight

- Give the sentence a real subject and a strong verb. Keep the subject close to the action when that makes the sentence easier to follow.
- Put familiar information before new information. Let each sentence give the reader a place to stand before asking them to absorb the next idea.
- Choose the precise word over the impressive one. A specific verb usually does more work than an adjective that announces importance.
- Keep a qualifier when it is true and useful. Replace vague uncertainty with the condition that explains when something works, fails, or changes.
- Use transitions to name the relationship between ideas. If the relationship is obvious, let the sentences meet without ceremony.
- Let sentence length follow the thought. Variety should come from the material, not from a rule about sounding natural.
- Use figurative language when it clarifies the subject or gives the reader a useful way to see it. Literal language is often the stronger choice.
- Keep the writer inside the subject. When a sentence explains how the reader should take the material—what needs explaining, what the “plain description” is, or why a detail is enough—replace it with the fact, action, or image underneath. Describe scale and structure concretely: name the number of songs, duration, sequence, or change instead of saying that a release “gives more room” or “has a longer shape.”

Read the draft aloud. The places where the voice becomes vague, ceremonious, or over-explanatory are usually audible before they are easy to name.

## Let voice come from judgment

Voice is not a list of adjectives such as warm, smart, conversational, or human. It is the pattern of decisions a writer makes: what they notice, what they believe is worth explaining, where they slow down, and what they refuse to exaggerate.

- Preserve the author's actual preferences, idioms, pacing, humor, and judgments when the source provides them.
- Treat first person as evidence of experience. Keep “I built,” “we found,” or “I changed” when it tells the reader something real. Do not add a personal history, feeling, or anecdote to create the appearance of a voice.
- Let personality enter through selection and precision. A writer does not need to label a detail as funny, moving, strange, or important; the detail may do that work itself.
- Let humor or an aside stay when it reveals something true about the subject. Do not add wit as a decorative layer.
- State the writer's judgment plainly and make it proportionate to the evidence. A modest conclusion drawn from a real example is stronger than a grand conclusion drawn from nothing.
- Keep uncertainty and failure when they are part of the author's thinking. “This worked in the normal case” or “I tried it and had to replace it” can carry more authority than a universal claim.
- When no personal material is available, write in the appropriate outside voice. Do not counterfeit intimacy.

Personal writing does not have to be casual or confessional. It has to contain a mind making choices about what is worth saying.

## Edit in passes

1. **Substance:** identify the claim, the evidence, and the reader's reason to care. Mark a paragraph as keep, tighten, or remove.
2. **Structure:** cut repetition, move the subject and main point forward, and give each paragraph a distinct job.
3. **Specificity:** add a supported detail, condition, example, or consequence wherever a surviving claim is still vague. If none exists, shorten or flag the passage.
4. **Sentences:** replace abstract nouns with clear actions where appropriate, make actors visible when they matter, and repair rhythm without imposing a house pattern.
5. **Voice:** restore the author's particular choices. Do not add personality after the fact; find it in the source or leave the prose appropriately plain.
6. **Final read:** apply the removal test. If deleting a sentence costs the reader no information, reasoning, image, or necessary rhythm, cut it.

Surface patterns are clues, not laws. A three-part list, a colon, an em dash, passive voice, a rhetorical question, or a first-person sentence can be exactly right. Judge whether the construction does work in this passage. Do not replace AI-sounding prose with a new set of mannerisms.

## Preserve what matters

Keep exact technical facts, dates, names, quotations, accessibility text, and legally necessary disclosures. Preserve the author's meaning. Do not invent numbers, examples, motives, experiences, or opinions to make a weak passage sound finished.

Public articles default to neutral or third-person prose. First person is welcome in an explicitly personal blog post, diary, or essay, and anywhere the source clearly speaks from experience. A project page can be personal without becoming a founder story.

The balance of simplicity, concrete examples, visible reasoning, and honest limits is usefully illustrated by Butler W. Lampson’s [*Hints for Computer System Design*](https://bwlampson.site/33-Hints/Acrobat.pdf). Borrow the method, not the wording.
